# Nursing educational-technology governance and procurement — 2026

## Purpose

Research bundle for understanding who actually decides to adopt a nursing educational technology product and how a nursing dean/program administrator interacts with faculty, simulation leaders, technology teams, finance, procurement and other institutional gatekeepers.

The key finding is that **buyer, curricular approver, budget owner, technical approver, contract signer, implementation owner and end user are frequently different people.**

## Nursing academic authority

### ACEN Standard 1

Source: https://www.acenursing.org/accreditation/accred-manual/standards/standard-1

ACEN requires formal governance participation from the nurse administrator and faculty. The nurse administrator has authority to lead the program, prepare its budget with faculty input and administer allocated fiscal resources.

This creates an important baseline: the nursing administrator is often a genuine economic and academic authority, not merely a figurehead. But the standard also embeds faculty participation, so curricular product adoption cannot be reduced to unilateral dean preference.

### ACEN multi-location governance

Source: https://www.acenursing.org/accreditation/accred-manual/glossary

For a single accredited nursing program delivered at multiple locations, ACEN describes one nurse administrator, one faculty body, a single set of student policies, and common evaluation processes. This can centralize academic decision rights across campuses even when implementation is distributed.

## Leadership structures observed

### Indiana University School of Nursing — multi-campus university matrix

Source: https://nursing.indianapolis.iu.edu/about/leadership/org-chart.html

Representative roles include:

- Dean;
- Executive Associate Dean of Clinical Academic Programs;
- Assistant Dean of Undergraduate Programs;
- Assistant Dean of Graduate Programs;
- Director of Clinical Placements;
- Assistant Dean for Learning Resources;
- Assistant Dean of Financial and Resource Management;
- local Associate Deans for Bloomington, Fort Wayne and Indianapolis.

This structure illustrates a central-academic/local-campus matrix: the dean is not the only meaningful product stakeholder. A simulation or learning product may intersect academic-program, clinical-placement, learning-resource, finance and campus leadership simultaneously.

### Johns Hopkins School of Nursing — research-intensive specialized leadership

Sources:
- https://e-catalogue.jhu.edu/nursing/
- https://nursing.jhu.edu/faculty-research/research/areas-of-expertise/policy-leadership-administration/the-center-for-simulation-and-immersive-learning/team/

Johns Hopkins lists a Dean and Executive Vice Dean alongside specialized associate deans for Finance and Administration, Simulation and Immersive Learning, Academic Affairs, Clinical Practice and Relationships, Enrollment Management, Faculty Development and other functions. The simulation center itself includes an Associate Dean, associate/director-level simulation education, simulation educators, a business program manager and simulation operations specialists.

This is a useful example of why a research-intensive school should not be modeled as 'dean + faculty.' Simulation strategy, finance, academic affairs, clinical relationships and operations have distinct senior owners.

### Blinn College — community-college health-sciences model

Sources:
- https://www.blinn.edu/organizational-chart/index.html
- https://www.blinn.edu/news/2026/04/director-of-simulation-earns-certification.html

At Blinn, the Dean of Health Sciences oversees the Director of Associate Degree Nursing, Director of Vocational Nursing and Director of Health Sciences Simulations, alongside several other health-professions program directors.

This structure changes the buying model materially:

- nursing is one program family inside a broader health-sciences portfolio;
- simulation leadership can serve multiple disciplines rather than nursing alone;
- the nursing director may own curricular need while a health-sciences dean owns cross-program resource tradeoffs.

### Holyoke Community College — shared health-education/simulation infrastructure

Source: https://www.hcc.edu/about/visit-hcc/center-for-health-education-%28che%29

Holyoke's Center for Health Education and Simulation houses nursing, health-sciences teaching and the simulation suite. Publicly listed roles include Dean of Education/Health Careers, Director of Nursing Education, PN and ASN chairs, Simulation Lab Coordinator and simulation technicians.

This again illustrates a leaner but still multi-role buying system: even at a community college, simulation operations, nursing curriculum and divisional budget leadership can be separate.

### Research-intensive / academic-health-center pattern

Large schools commonly distribute responsibility among associate deans for academic affairs, finance/administration, simulation or clinical learning, student affairs and program leadership. The greater the specialization, the less useful a single-title 'dean buyer' model becomes.

### Community-college health-sciences pattern

At community colleges, nursing may sit below a broader Dean of Health Sciences or allied-health division. Nursing directors/program administrators often carry regulatory and curricular accountability while simulation resources may be shared across nursing and other health programmes.

In this model, the highest economic authority may be above nursing even when the nursing director is the essential academic sponsor.

### National multi-campus pattern

National institutions can combine central curriculum, regulatory and product standards with local campus presidents/deans and local clinical-learning operations. This creates a matrix in which enterprise technology may be selected centrally but campus adoption quality depends on local leadership, faculty and clinical operations.

## Technology-procurement gates outside nursing

### University of Kansas

Sources:
- https://provost.ku.edu/memos/20241105-0
- https://procurement.ku.edu/procurement-compliance-guidance

KU requires joint Procurement and IT review for software purchases regardless of cost, funding source or payment method. Reviews address cybersecurity, accessibility, duplicate capability and support requirements. Security review may use HECVAT and can examine FERPA, HIPAA, GDPR, authentication, continuity and incident response.

This shows why a dean with budget authority still cannot simply 'buy the software' in many universities.

### University of Michigan

Source: https://spg.umich.edu/policy/601.03-3

U-M's software/cloud-services policy applies across academic, teaching, learning, clinical and research activities and to software obtained throughout the university. Procurement and security requirements vary with data and risk.

### University at Buffalo

Sources:
- https://www.buffalo.edu/access/digital/procurement.html
- https://www.buffalo.edu/administrative-services/managing-procurement/commonly-purchased-goods/information-technology/software.html

UB explicitly states that review may begin when an individual faculty/staff member identifies a software need. The subsequent review can involve:

- Financial Management for payment/student-fee issues;
- accessibility/EDI review;
- CIO/security/privacy/integration review;
- Purchasing for agreements, contracts, terms and conditions;
- legal review depending on contract complexity.

This is a strong public example of a **distributed buying group** beginning with faculty need and ending with institutional approval.

## Generalized nursing ed-tech buying path

The sources support the following model. The exact order and titles vary by institution.

### 1. Need formation

Potential initiators:

- course faculty or course coordinator;
- simulation director/educator;
- program director;
- clinical-placement leader;
- assessment/accreditation leader;
- dean/nurse administrator;
- students or support teams surfacing a learning problem.

Typical triggers:

- inconsistent clinical exposure;
- a competency gap;
- accreditation/evaluation need;
- faculty workload;
- clinical placement scarcity;
- NCLEX/readiness concerns;
- replacement of an incumbent product;
- new modality/campus/program launch.

### 2. Academic/curricular legitimacy

Questions include:

- Which course/program outcomes does this support?
- Is it an assignment, a course component, a clinical/simulation activity, or a curriculum change?
- Who evaluates student performance?
- Does use require curriculum-committee approval?
- Does it align with regulator/accreditor expectations?

Course-level tools may move with substantial faculty discretion. Program-wide competency or clinical-learning systems usually require broader academic governance.

### 3. Sponsor and pilot

A faculty champion, simulation leader or program leader often becomes the operational sponsor. A pilot may establish learner/faculty usability, curriculum fit and local outcomes before wider adoption.

### 4. Budget decision

Possible economic owners include:

- nursing dean/nurse administrator;
- health-sciences dean;
- associate dean for finance/administration;
- central academic affairs;
- campus/system leadership.

Funding routes vary: school operating budget, institutional contract, student course/material fee, direct learner purchase, grant or special initiative.

### 5. Institutional technology and procurement gates

Depending on product risk and institution, approval can include:

- information security/privacy;
- FERPA and, where relevant, HIPAA/data-classification review;
- accessibility/VPAT review;
- LMS/SSO/integration architecture;
- AI/algorithm governance;
- duplicate-software review;
- purchasing/competitive-bid rules;
- legal/vendor terms;
- finance/student-fee rules.

### 6. Implementation

Implementation owners can include course faculty, simulation educators, instructional designers, LMS/learning-resources staff, simulation technologists and vendor success teams. The person who approved the spend may have little involvement in weekly use.

### 7. Evidence and renewal

Renewal can depend on a different coalition than initial adoption. Faculty satisfaction, student outcomes, support burden, usage, accessibility/security compliance, budget and strategic alignment all become relevant.

## Implication for Shadow Health research

The most useful commercial unit is not 'the dean' but the **institutional buying system**.

For each account, research should resolve at minimum:

- academic sponsor;
- faculty/curriculum approver;
- simulation/clinical-learning owner;
- economic buyer/budget holder;
- IT/security/accessibility approvers;
- procurement/legal path;
- implementation owner;
- learner payment model;
- renewal decision-maker.

A CRM that stores only one buyer title will systematically obscure adoption risk and expansion opportunity.
