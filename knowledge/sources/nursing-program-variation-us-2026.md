# U.S. nursing-program and campus variation — 2026

## Purpose

Research bundle for understanding which differences among U.S. nursing programs and campuses materially change the learner journey, simulation strategy, implementation model, or educational-technology buying process.

This source deliberately separates **program architecture** from **campus implementation**. A campus can share curriculum and governance with other campuses while differing materially in clinical partners, faculty capacity, simulation access, student travel burden, and local operations.

## National regulatory and accreditation context

### NCSBN — approval of nursing education programs

Source: https://ncsbn.org/nursing-regulation/education/approval-of-nursing-education-programs.page

Nursing regulatory bodies approve prelicensure nursing programs because graduation from an approved program is part of eligibility to take the NCLEX. Program approval therefore creates a jurisdiction-specific layer beyond institutional and nursing-program accreditation.

### ACEN — Standard 1: Administrative Capacity and Resources

Source: https://www.acenursing.org/accreditation/accred-manual/standards/standard-1

Durable observations:

- the nurse administrator and nursing faculty must have formal representation in governance;
- the nurse administrator has authority to lead the nursing program, prepare the budget with faculty input, and administer allocated fiscal resources;
- fiscal and physical resources must be sufficient and sustainable across locations and delivery methods.

This matters commercially because a nursing dean/director may control or strongly influence the nursing-program budget while still operating inside broader institutional procurement, IT, accessibility, legal, and finance controls.

### ACEN — single-program / multi-location structure

Source: https://www.acenursing.org/accreditation/accred-manual/glossary

ACEN describes conditions under which multiple locations function as one nursing program: one nurse administrator with responsibility and authority at all locations, one faculty group functioning as a whole, a single set of policies, and a common systematic evaluation plan. ACEN can determine whether a location needs separate accreditation.

This supports an important distinction: **multiple campuses do not necessarily mean multiple independent nursing programs or multiple independent product-buying units.**

## Capacity pressure

### AACN — Nursing Faculty Shortage Fact Sheet

Source: https://www.aacnnursing.org/news-data/fact-sheets/nursing-faculty-shortage

AACN reports that U.S. baccalaureate and graduate nursing programs turned away 92,672 qualified applications in 2025 because of constraints including faculty, clinical sites, classroom space, preceptors, and budget. AACN also reported 1,588 full-time faculty vacancies across 863 responding schools and a 7.2% vacancy rate.

For simulation strategy, the important point is not merely that demand is high. Program capacity is constrained by several resources that simulation may affect differently: simulation can increase access to standardized practice, but it does not automatically solve faculty, physical-space, regulatory, or direct-care placement constraints.

## Multi-campus public university example — Indiana University

### Shared curriculum with local campus variation

Source: https://nursing.indianapolis.iu.edu/academics/programs-degree/bsn/traditional-track/index.html

IU Bloomington, Fort Wayne, and Indianapolis each offer the full BSN curriculum. Nursing courses can be required in different sequences or semesters, and clinical experiences can differ by campus. Some courses are location-specific.

### Campus environments

Source: https://nursing.iu.edu/locations/index.html

IU characterizes the campuses differently:

- Bloomington: college-town environment with close faculty connections and community learning;
- Fort Wayne: regional health-care hub with regional provider partnerships;
- Indianapolis: located in Indiana's largest health-care and life-sciences center, near major hospitals and research institutions.

The material product implication is that common curriculum does not produce a common clinical-learning environment.

### Leadership structure

Source: https://nursing.indianapolis.iu.edu/about/leadership/org-chart.html

IU has one dean with an executive academic structure that includes an Executive Associate Dean of Clinical Academic Programs, undergraduate and graduate program leaders, a Director of Clinical Placements, an Assistant Dean for Learning Resources, financial/resource leadership, and local associate deans for Bloomington, Fort Wayne, and Indianapolis.

This is a useful example of a **central academic / local campus matrix**: product selection and curriculum strategy can be centralized while implementation depends on campus leaders and local operating conditions.

## Distributed hybrid prelicensure example — Western Governors University

Source: https://www.wgu.edu/online-nursing-health-degrees/rn-prelicensure-nursing-bachelors-program.html

WGU's prelicensure BSN combines:

- online coursework;
- two in-person learning-lab experiences at regional Clinical Learning and Simulation Centers;
- seven in-person clinical rotations at assigned health-care partners;
- state- and region-dependent clinical assignment rules;
- virtual-reality and high-fidelity simulation before clinical progression.

Students may travel significant distances to simulation centers and clinical districts. Clinical assignments depend on state of residence, student status, and capacity.

This archetype shows that simulation can become a **regional network resource rather than a campus building**, and geographic logistics become part of the learner experience and cost structure.

## National multi-campus example — Chamberlain University

Source: https://www.chamberlain.edu/academics/academic-resources/curriculum-and-catalogs

Chamberlain publishes a common three-year BSN curriculum for campuses across many U.S. states. A centralized curriculum can therefore coexist with many local regulatory jurisdictions and clinical markets.

The product-research implication is that the same academic product may need one enterprise relationship but multiple jurisdictional, campus-implementation, and clinical-context configurations.

## Community-college / health-sciences model

Representative public organizational structures reviewed during this research include community colleges where nursing is housed under a broader health-sciences dean and the nursing director/program administrator sits below that level. Simulation leadership may serve several health professions rather than nursing alone.

This differs from a standalone school or college of nursing in three material ways:

1. the nursing administrator may not be the highest budget authority for the unit;
2. simulation resources may be shared with allied-health programs;
3. purchasing may compete with other health-program priorities inside one school/division budget.

## Material U.S. variation dimensions

The evidence supports treating these as high-value segmentation variables:

- **program type:** ADN, traditional BSN, accelerated BSN, graduate-entry prelicensure, RN-to-BSN, MSN, DNP/APRN;
- **regulatory jurisdiction:** state board rules and whether a program operates across states;
- **governance structure:** independent school, health-sciences division, multi-campus system, national institution;
- **curriculum control:** campus-local versus centrally standardized;
- **clinical market:** number/type of local placement partners and competition for sites/preceptors;
- **simulation infrastructure:** dedicated center, shared interprofessional center, regional hub, mobile lab, primarily virtual model;
- **faculty and preceptor capacity:** staffing levels and development burden;
- **cohort scale and throughput:** learner volume and number of simultaneous sections/locations;
- **delivery model:** residential, commuter, hybrid, online/distributed;
- **student population:** traditional undergraduate, working RN, rural/distributed learner, accelerated second-degree learner;
- **funding model:** institutional license, course fee, learner-purchased access, grant-supported, system contract;
- **technology governance:** campus/system IT, accessibility, privacy/security and procurement gates.

## Material versus non-material campus variation

A campus difference is strategically material when it changes at least one of the following:

- what learners can reliably experience;
- what faculty must operationalize;
- what can count toward curriculum or clinical learning;
- implementation cost or logistics;
- product configuration/integration;
- who must approve or fund the product;
- outcomes the program must demonstrate.

Differences in campus brand, architecture, or general student-life identity are usually weak segmentation variables unless they proxy for a meaningful educational or operating difference.
