# Nursing student motivations, life conditions and career formation — 2026

## Purpose

This source bundle looks beyond enrollment demographics to the conditions under which nursing learners study, why people choose nursing, how career identity forms, and which pressures matter for learning-product design.

## Why people choose nursing

### 2025 global scoping review

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40220457/

A 2025 scoping review synthesized 49 studies published from 2014-2024. It grouped influences on choosing nursing into six themes:

1. personal desire;
2. social influence;
3. career opportunities;
4. job flexibility;
5. characteristics of the nursing education program;
6. pandemic-related influences.

The important point is that nursing career choice is usually **multi-causal**. Altruism and desire to help coexist with pragmatic considerations such as employment security, flexibility, mobility, compensation, program accessibility, and influence from family or nurses already known to the student.

### Second-degree and graduate-entry learners

Source: https://www.aacnnursing.org/news-data/fact-sheets/accelerated-baccalaureate-and-masters-degrees-in-nursing

Second-degree learners have already completed a bachelor's degree and often have previous work or professional experience. Their decision to enter nursing can therefore be a deliberate career redirection rather than an initial career selection. Published research on graduate-entry and second-degree students repeatedly identifies meaning/purpose, desire for more satisfying work, career stability, prior healthcare exposure and the ability to enter a new profession through an accelerated pathway as recurring motives.

The learner can be **adult-experienced but clinically novice**. Instruction that assumes general immaturity simply because clinical knowledge is novice-level risks misreading this population.

## Nursing school is often lived alongside other adult obligations

### Financial stress

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40590434/

A 2025 systematic integrative review found that financial stress materially affects nursing-student mental health and wellbeing. Stress arose from personal, academic and clinical sources and varied across the educational journey. Marginalized and underserved learners experienced disproportionate effects.

Clinical education has a particular economic structure: required placements consume substantial time but are commonly unpaid, which can reduce the hours available for paid employment at the same time that learners face tuition, transportation, uniforms/equipment and living costs.

The review argues that financial stress can compound rather than remain isolated: reducing paid employment can improve study time while worsening financial strain; working more can improve cash flow while worsening time, fatigue and academic pressure.

### Burnout

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39120506/

A 2024 meta-analysis covering 34 studies and 9,554 nursing students estimated pooled academic burnout prevalence at 35%, with high emotional exhaustion at 40%. These are global pooled estimates rather than a U.S.-specific prevalence rate, but they reinforce that learner load is not a peripheral issue.

For simulation design, a high-fidelity or pedagogically rich activity is not automatically learner-centered if it adds substantial preparation, scheduling or cognitive burden without clearly improving competence.

## Adult and working-learner pathways

### RN-to-BSN

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32222567/

A multi-program U.S. study of online RN-to-BSN learners explicitly treated these programs as education for working nurses. Learners valued clinically relevant topics such as evidence-based practice, public health, pathophysiology, pharmacology, leadership, assessment and informatics. They were less enthusiastic about generic discussion-board and group-project work, and extracurricular life responsibilities affected participation.

The small sample means the specific preference rankings should not be treated as nationally representative. The durable signal is stronger: already-licensed nurses evaluate school against the opportunity cost of work and home responsibilities and are especially sensitive to whether activities feel relevant to practice.

### DNP learners

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26866732/

A nationwide survey of 172 DNP students and graduates reported a mean age of 46.2 years; 83% worked full time. Major barriers were competing work, family and school demands. Most reported feeling overwhelmed and 37% had considered quitting.

This is older evidence and should not be generalized to every DNP program, but it clearly illustrates why the graduate learner cannot be modeled as a residential student with school as the dominant life commitment.

AACN's DNP research similarly reports that many DNP students work full time and that cost, work demands and competing life priorities are major completion challenges:
https://www.aacnnursing.org/Portals/0/PDFs/Data/State-of-the-DNP-Summary-Report-June-2022.pdf

## Career identity is shaped during school, not fixed at admission

### Clinical-placement influence

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38493537/

A 2024 study of final-year nursing students found low confidence in career decision-making among 53% of participants and identified clinical environment, educational experiences and individual characteristics as major influences on specialty choice. The study was conducted in Western Australia, so its numerical findings should not be transplanted into the U.S.; the mechanism is nevertheless useful.

Clinical placements do more than teach competence. They reveal specialties, role models, team cultures and working conditions and can make a previously abstract nursing specialty feel attractive or undesirable.

This suggests that simulation can have a secondary role in **career discovery**, especially where a learner's real clinical placements are too narrow to expose them to the breadth of nursing practice. That product implication remains a hypothesis rather than an established effect of Shadow Health.

## Learning motivation inside simulation

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40539177/

A 2025 scoping review of 21 studies found simulation-based learning associated with intrinsic motivation, self-efficacy, critical thinking and engagement. Motivating features included:

- realism;
- repetition;
- reflection;
- structured debriefing;
- emotional engagement.

The review also noted limited adaptability to diverse learners as a challenge. This matters for a population whose prior experience ranges from first-semester undergraduate to an RN with a decade of practice returning for graduate education.

## A useful distinction: novice status versus adult status

Nursing education repeatedly produces combinations that conventional student personas blur:

- young adult + clinical novice: common traditional BSN learner;
- older adult + clinical novice: career-changing accelerated/graduate-entry learner;
- adult + clinically experienced + academically returning: RN-to-BSN learner;
- established expert in one nursing scope + novice in a new scope: NP, CRNA, CNS or other advanced-practice learner;
- experienced clinician + novice systems leader/researcher/educator: many DNP, MSN leadership/education or PhD learners.

Instructional personalization should therefore separate **life experience, nursing experience, specialty experience, academic experience and current target scope** instead of treating expertise as a single ladder.

## What remains under-measured

Public evidence is much stronger on stress and broad motivation than on the everyday household structure of U.S. nursing students. We still need better current evidence by pathway on:

- percentage employed while enrolled and weekly hours worked;
- dependent-care responsibilities;
- schedule instability caused by clinical placements;
- financial contribution from spouses/partners/family;
- employer tuition support;
- commuting/travel burden;
- how learners choose among ADN, BSN, accelerated and graduate-entry routes;
- how specialty intentions change after simulation versus real clinical exposure.
