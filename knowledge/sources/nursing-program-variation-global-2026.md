# International nursing-program and simulation variation — 2026

## Purpose

Research bundle for distinguishing durable features of nursing education from U.S.-specific assumptions, especially around practice hours, simulation, regulation, and the role simulation can legitimately claim in a curriculum.

## United Kingdom

### Nursing and Midwifery Council — standards for pre-registration nursing programmes

Source: https://www.nmc.org.uk/standards/standards-for-nurses/standards-for-pre-registration-nursing-programmes/

The NMC organizes standards around the student journey: selection/admission/progression, curriculum, practice learning, supervision/assessment, and qualification. Approved education institutions and practice-learning partners jointly deliver programmes.

### NMC — simulated practice learning

Source: https://www.nmc.org.uk/education/becoming-a-nurse-midwife-nursing-associate/simulated-practice-learning/

Current NMC policy requires 2,300 practice-learning hours for pre-registration nursing programmes and permits up to 600 of those hours to be delivered through simulated practice learning when requirements are met. Simulation can include actors, immersive environments and virtual reality and must be designed, supervised and assessed as meaningful practice learning.

**Product implication:** the UK supports an explicit category of simulation that can carry recognised practice-learning weight. A vendor can therefore have a stronger regulatory-hours story than in jurisdictions where simulation is explicitly excluded from required clinical practice hours.

## Australia

### Australian Nursing and Midwifery Accreditation Council — Registered Nurse Accreditation Standards evidence guide

Source: https://anmac.org.au/sites/default/files/2025-10/anmac_rnas2019_evidence_guide_updated_2025.pdf

The current evidence guide requires entry-to-practice RN programmes to include simulated learning sequenced to prepare students for professional experience placement (PEP). It also requires a minimum of 800 hours of quality PEP, **exclusive of simulation**, across a variety of settings.

**Product implication:** simulation is structurally important, but its clearest regulatory job is preparation, rehearsal and competency development around real clinical placement rather than replacement of the required 800 placement hours.

## New Zealand

### Nursing Council of New Zealand — education programme standards

Source: https://www.nursingcouncil.org.nz/Public/Education/Standards_for_programmes.aspx

Current standards require at least 1,000 clinical hours in registered-nurse education and 700 in enrolled-nurse education. The Council has stated that simulation is excluded from those minimum clinical-hour totals because education providers already use simulation throughout programmes to prepare students for clinical learning.

**Product implication:** as in Australia, the portable value proposition is preparation, standardisation, deliberate practice and evidence of competence—not a broad claim that virtual simulation replaces mandated clinical hours.

## European Union

### Directive 2005/36/EC / consolidated professional-qualification rules

Source: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02005L0036-20240303

Training for nurses responsible for general care is harmonised around minimum duration requirements: at least three years and 4,600 hours of theoretical and clinical training, with theoretical training at least one-third and clinical training at least one-half of the minimum duration. Member states retain responsibility for organizing education within this framework.

The EU framework therefore creates a stronger common floor for direct clinical training than the state-by-state U.S. model, while implementation and educational technology decisions remain country/institution specific.

## Canada

### Canadian Association of Schools of Nursing — National Nursing Education Framework and simulation guidance

Source: https://www.casn.ca/education/education-home/

CASN provides national educational guidance and treats its Clinical Placements and Simulation Guidelines as a companion to the practice domain of the National Nursing Education Framework. CASN competencies complement, rather than replace, entry-to-practice competencies established by regulatory bodies.

Canada therefore has a national education-quality layer but provincial/territorial regulation remains material. Product research should not treat Canada as one regulatory simulation policy.

### British Columbia — student clinical supervision example

Source: https://www.bccnm.ca/RN/learning/regulatorysupervision/Pages/students.aspx

BCCNM frames student participation in client care through regulatory supervision: nurses decide what students can safely do, set supervision conditions and adjust them as risk changes. This illustrates that real-patient practice is not simply 'exposure'; it carries a professional supervisory relationship that simulation does not reproduce exactly.

## Singapore

### National University of Singapore — Bachelor of Science (Nursing)

Source: https://medicine.nus.edu.sg/nursing/education/bachelor-of-science-nursing-bsc-nursing/

NUS describes a three-year progression in which clinical practicum integrates on-campus clinical laboratory and simulation experience with clinical attachments. Learners build foundations, consolidate nursing knowledge and practice, then transition toward professional practice.

Singapore is useful as a counterexample to overly Western institutional assumptions: a relatively concentrated health system and university landscape can produce tighter coordination among education and clinical partners than the fragmented U.S. clinical-placement market.

## What appears universal across systems

Across the jurisdictions reviewed, nursing education consistently contains:

- a progression from theoretical/disciplinary knowledge toward increasingly integrated professional practice;
- supervised real-patient clinical learning;
- formal assessment of competence/readiness;
- regulatory and/or professional-body oversight;
- an institutional obligation to ensure appropriate clinical experiences;
- simulation as a safe, controlled and repeatable environment for preparation, integration, rare events and feedback.

## What varies enough to change product strategy

The largest cross-national differences are:

- required practice/clinical hours;
- whether simulation can count toward those hours;
- regulator versus accreditor roles;
- entry pathways and degree structure;
- clinical supervision and placement models;
- centralization of the health-care/education system;
- institution-level procurement and technology governance;
- language, cultural and scope-of-practice expectations.

## Cross-market product lesson

'Clinical-hour replacement' is a jurisdiction-specific proposition. **Competency development, standardized exposure, deliberate practice, feedback, remediation and readiness evidence are more portable global product jobs.**

The product should therefore separate the educational value of simulation from any jurisdiction-specific claim about regulatory substitution or practice-hour credit.
