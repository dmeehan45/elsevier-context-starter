# Nursing program archetypes and material variation — 2026

## Why this map exists

'College of nursing' is too broad a customer category for product strategy. Two institutions can teach toward similar entry-to-practice outcomes while operating very different educational systems.

The useful segmentation question is not simply **what degree do they offer?** It is:

> What combination of regulation, clinical-learning supply, organizational structure, simulation capability, learner population and decision rights determines how this program develops competence?

That operating model predicts where simulation can create value, how difficult implementation will be, and who must participate in adoption.

## U.S. program archetypes

### 1. Community-college ADN / practical-nursing program

Typical shape:

- nursing may sit inside a broader health-sciences division;
- a nursing director/program administrator carries program/regulatory responsibility;
- health-sciences dean may hold broader budget authority;
- faculty teams are relatively lean;
- simulation labs may serve nursing plus allied-health programs;
- clinical relationships are intensely local;
- students are often commuters with substantial work/family constraints.

Simulation product jobs:

- guarantee exposure when local clinical opportunities are uneven;
- create repeatable practice without adding placement sites;
- support small faculty teams with structured content/remediation;
- help use limited lab time more efficiently.

Commercial implication: lower organizational complexity does **not** automatically mean simpler buying. Budgets can be tight, student-fee sensitivity can be high, and the nursing champion may need approval from a broader health-sciences or college administration.

### 2. Single-campus university BSN school

Typical shape:

- dean or nursing-school administrator;
- undergraduate program director/associate dean;
- course coordinators and faculty governance;
- dedicated or shared simulation center;
- clinical placement staff;
- university IT/procurement outside the school.

Simulation product jobs:

- curriculum-integrated practice from assessment through specialty care;
- competency tracking and remediation;
- standardize cases that are unreliable in clinical rotations;
- connect didactic learning to bedside judgment.

Commercial implication: the dean may have budget authority, but faculty/course leaders determine whether the product becomes part of actual learning rather than shelfware.

### 3. Research-intensive school / academic health center

Typical shape:

- dean plus multiple associate/executive deans;
- separate undergraduate, graduate and advanced-practice leadership;
- specialized simulation/immersive-learning leadership;
- clinical placement, assessment, learning technology and finance teams;
- strong university/hospital IT, privacy, procurement and legal functions;
- multiple degree programs and research priorities.

Simulation product jobs:

- span multiple programs while preserving course-specific fit;
- support sophisticated competency assessment and analytics;
- interoperate with a larger educational technology ecosystem;
- meet higher expectations for evidence, integration and accessibility.

Commercial implication: these are **buying systems**, not dean-led transactions. More stakeholders increase potential enterprise value but also increase evaluation and implementation complexity.

### 4. Multi-campus public university/system program

Indiana University illustrates the pattern: one BSN curriculum and centralized school leadership across Bloomington, Fort Wayne and Indianapolis, with local associate deans, different course sequencing and potentially different clinical experiences.

Typical shape:

- one central curriculum and program evaluation system;
- central dean/nurse administrator;
- campus associate deans or local program leaders;
- common learning outcomes with local delivery variance;
- local clinical markets and faculty resources.

Simulation product jobs:

- create a common competency/practice layer across uneven clinical environments;
- make learner evidence comparable across campuses;
- allow local implementation without fragmenting central curricular intent.

Commercial implication: **the contract boundary may be central while the adoption boundary is local.** Campus-level usage variation should not automatically be interpreted as different product-market fit; it may be an implementation-capacity problem.

### 5. National multi-campus institution

Examples include institutions such as Chamberlain or Galen, where curricula and academic standards can be centrally governed across multiple states while campuses maintain local leadership and clinical relationships.

Typical shape:

- central academic/curriculum leadership;
- national regulatory/accreditation functions;
- local campus deans/presidents;
- local clinical-learning teams;
- matrix accountability between campus and national functions.

Simulation product jobs:

- provide national standardization;
- support rapid campus/program expansion;
- create comparable learner data across locations;
- adapt implementation to state regulation and local clinical supply.

Commercial implication: an enterprise adoption can be extremely valuable, but product configuration needs to separate **central standard** from **local operational context**.

### 6. Distributed / hybrid prelicensure program

WGU is a strong example: online coursework, regional simulation centers and geographically distributed clinical placements.

Typical shape:

- digital didactic experience;
- regional simulation hubs rather than a traditional campus lab;
- clinical rotations distributed through partner networks;
- substantial student travel/logistics;
- state-by-state approval and placement constraints.

Simulation product jobs:

- provide practice before learners travel to clinical intensives;
- reduce dependence on physical lab access for cognitive/communication practice;
- create continuity across geographically separated experiences;
- help gate progression into scarce in-person experiences.

Commercial implication: 'campus' becomes the wrong unit of analysis. Geography, state regulation, regional sim infrastructure and clinical-partner capacity are more useful.

### 7. Postlicensure online RN-to-BSN / MSN / DNP

Typical shape:

- learners are already licensed and often employed;
- high demand for asynchronous/flexible learning;
- RN-to-BSN may emphasize population health, leadership, evidence and assessment rather than foundational bedside skill acquisition;
- APRN programs add advanced assessment, diagnostics, management and required practicum/preceptor experiences.

Simulation product jobs:

- RN-to-BSN: advanced assessment, communication, population/community reasoning where relevant;
- APRN: differential diagnosis, advanced assessment, treatment planning, telehealth and OSCE-like competency practice.

Commercial implication: the same 'graduate nursing' label hides very different practice requirements and value propositions.

## The campus-variation hierarchy

Not every campus difference deserves product segmentation. Use this hierarchy.

### Tier 1 — changes the permissible educational model

- state/country regulation;
- accreditation status/scope;
- whether simulation can count toward mandated practice hours;
- program approval/licensure requirements.

These can change what the product is legally or educationally allowed to do.

### Tier 2 — changes the supply of learning experiences

- local clinical-site supply;
- competition for placements/preceptors;
- nearby specialty services;
- rural versus major academic-medical-center environment;
- simulation-center capacity and equipment.

These change which experiences learners can obtain reliably.

### Tier 3 — changes implementation economics

- cohort size and schedule;
- faculty-to-student capacity;
- faculty simulation expertise;
- instructional-design/technology support;
- student travel burden;
- local versus central operational staffing.

These change whether an otherwise good product can be used well.

### Tier 4 — changes decision rights

- local versus central curriculum control;
- local versus system budget;
- shared versus campus-specific procurement;
- centralized versus distributed simulation leadership.

These change who must say yes and who can cause implementation to fail.

### Tier 5 — low-value segmentation

- campus identity or prestige by itself;
- physical building aesthetics;
- generic urban/suburban labels without evidence that they change clinical supply, learner population or operations.

These may be descriptively interesting but should not be allowed to masquerade as product-relevant differences.

## International variation

### United States

The U.S. combines state regulatory approval, optional/required nursing accreditation depending on context, institutional governance and highly local clinical-placement markets. Simulation substitution rules vary by jurisdiction. This produces a fragmented implementation environment.

### United Kingdom

Pre-registration learners complete 2,300 practice-learning hours; current NMC standards permit up to 600 hours as qualifying simulated practice learning. Simulation therefore can be designed as a formally recognized component of practice learning when contextualized, supervised and quality-assured.

### Australia

Entry-to-practice RN programs include simulated learning to prepare learners for professional experience placement, but the required minimum 800 hours of professional experience placement excludes simulation.

### New Zealand

Registered-nurse programs require at least 1,000 clinical hours and enrolled-nurse programs 700; simulation is excluded from those minimums because it is already used throughout programs to prepare learners for clinical learning.

### European Union

General-care nurse education is harmonized around at least three years / 4,600 hours of theoretical and clinical training, with clinical training comprising at least half of the minimum duration. National education systems still control implementation.

### Canada

CASN provides national education and simulation guidance, while entry-to-practice regulation remains provincial/territorial. Canada should therefore be modeled as a shared professional-education framework layered over jurisdictional regulatory variation.

### Singapore

NUS provides an example of integrated progression across campus clinical labs/simulation and clinical attachments in a comparatively concentrated national health ecosystem.

## Universal product jobs versus local regulatory claims

### Globally portable

- safe deliberate practice;
- standardized patient/case exposure;
- rare-event exposure;
- feedback and debriefing;
- remediation and repetition;
- competency evidence;
- preparation before direct patient care;
- continuity across uneven clinical experiences.

### Jurisdiction-specific

- replacement of clinical/practice hours;
- exact proportion of simulation permitted;
- whether a modality qualifies as regulated practice learning;
- faculty/supervisor requirements;
- assessment use and documentation required for approval/accreditation.

## Strategic implication for Shadow Health

The most promising segmentation frame is a matrix, not a list of degree names:

**learner level × regulatory model × clinical scarcity × organizational topology × simulation maturity × delivery geography × buying-system complexity.**

That gives us a better explanation for why the same Shadow Health collection can be indispensable at one school, peripheral at another and operationally difficult at a third even when all three teach ostensibly the same BSN course.
