# Nursing educational-technology buying and leadership map — 2026

## Core model

A nursing educational-technology purchase is rarely one buyer making one decision.

The useful unit is a **buying system** with distinct decision rights:

| Decision right | Typical roles | Question they answer |
|---|---|---|
| Problem owner | faculty, course coordinator, simulation leader, program director | What learning/operating problem are we solving? |
| Curricular authority | faculty group, curriculum committee, program director, associate dean | Does this belong in the curriculum and how will it be used? |
| Nursing accountable executive | dean, nurse administrator, health-sciences dean | Is this strategically and financially worth supporting? |
| Simulation/clinical owner | simulation director, clinical-placement leader, lab faculty | Does it fit the practice-learning system? |
| Economic buyer | dean/nurse administrator, finance/administrative dean, central academic unit | Which budget or fee model pays? |
| Technical gatekeeper | IT/security/privacy/accessibility/LMS teams | Is it safe, accessible, supportable and integrable? |
| Commercial gatekeeper | procurement, legal, finance | Can we contract on acceptable terms and process? |
| Implementation owner | faculty, instructional design, sim team, vendor success | Can it become routine teaching practice? |
| Renewal coalition | budget owner + faculty/program leadership + institutional gatekeepers | Is continued use worth cost and burden? |

One person may occupy several roles in a small program. Large schools can assign almost every row to a different person or team.

## Leadership archetypes

### A. Lean community-college program

Common structure:

**college administration / health-sciences dean → nursing director/program administrator → course faculty**

with a small simulation/lab team, sometimes shared across health professions.

Likely adoption dynamic:

- faculty or nursing director identifies need;
- nursing faculty establishes curricular fit;
- nursing director sponsors;
- health-sciences dean or higher administrator may approve budget;
- central college IT/procurement still clears software.

Risk: a vendor over-indexes on the dean and misses that a small number of faculty have enormous influence over whether the product is genuinely adopted.

### B. Standalone university school of nursing

Common structure:

**dean → associate dean/program director → course coordinators/faculty**, with simulation, clinical placement and finance/operations functions.

Likely adoption dynamic:

- course or program need forms below dean level;
- faculty/curriculum governance establishes legitimacy;
- program leadership and simulation team shape implementation;
- dean/finance function funds program-wide use;
- central university gates approve technology and contract.

Risk: interpreting dean sponsorship as sufficient evidence that faculty workflow has changed.

### C. Research-intensive academic health center

Common structure:

**dean + executive/associate deans + program directors + simulation/clinical/assessment/learning-tech leaders + central university/health-system functions**.

Likely adoption dynamic:

- multiple stakeholders can initiate or block;
- stronger demand for evidence, integrations, data governance and accessibility;
- pilots may occur in one course/program before enterprise adoption;
- health-system relationships may influence clinical/simulation strategy.

Risk: the 'buyer' may appear to move during the sales cycle because different decision rights surface at different phases.

### D. Multi-campus public school/system

Common structure:

**central dean/nurse administrator → central academic/program functions → local associate dean/campus leadership → local faculty/clinical operations**.

Likely adoption dynamic:

- central selection can establish common product/curriculum;
- campus implementation varies with local faculty capacity, clinical sites and simulation resources;
- data comparability across campuses may be a central value proposition.

Risk: treating weak use at one campus as a failed enterprise value proposition when the root cause is local implementation.

### E. National multi-campus institution

Common structure:

**national academic/curriculum/regulatory leadership ↔ campus dean/president ↔ local faculty/clinical learning**, often with matrix or dotted-line functions.

Likely adoption dynamic:

- central standards and scale favor enterprise selection;
- campus leadership controls or heavily influences execution;
- state-specific regulation and clinical markets still constrain local delivery.

Risk: building a product configuration around headquarters assumptions that does not survive campus-level clinical reality.

### F. Distributed/online program

Common structure:

central digital curriculum and academic leadership combined with regional simulation/clinical networks.

Likely adoption dynamic:

- digital learning fit and integration are especially important;
- simulation may support readiness gates before scarce in-person intensives;
- state authorization and clinical geography can constrain scaling.

Risk: using a campus-centric account model when the true operating units are regions, states, clinical districts or learner cohorts.

## The dean's actual role

The dean or nurse administrator is often important for three reasons:

1. **accountability:** program quality, accreditation/approval and outcomes ultimately roll upward;
2. **resource allocation:** ACEN explicitly expects the nurse administrator to prepare the nursing-program budget with faculty input and administer allocated resources;
3. **strategic legitimacy:** enterprise or multi-course products need a leader willing to make them part of the program operating model.

But 'dean = buyer' is an incomplete model.

The dean usually cannot substitute for:

- faculty acceptance of curricular fit;
- simulation/clinical expertise;
- university security/privacy/accessibility approval;
- procurement/legal contracting;
- implementation capacity.

The more consequential and integrated the product, the more the dean behaves like an **executive sponsor and resource allocator inside a coalition**, not a solo purchaser.

## How product scope changes the buying group

### Course assignment / narrow virtual patient module

Likely center of gravity: course coordinator + faculty.

A product can sometimes be adopted as required courseware or student-purchased material with relatively localized academic decision-making, although institutional software rules can still apply.

### Multi-course curriculum product

Likely center of gravity: program director/associate dean + faculty curriculum governance + dean/economic buyer.

Now the vendor must prove coherence across courses, faculty usability and student progression value.

### Simulation-center platform

Likely center of gravity: simulation leadership + academic leadership + operations/technology + dean.

Physical workflows, scheduling, facilitation, debriefing and technology support matter as much as content.

### Competency/readiness analytics platform

Likely center of gravity: dean/program leadership + assessment/accreditation + faculty + institutional data/IT.

Because the product claims to create longitudinal evidence about learner performance, data governance, validity and interpretation become higher-stakes.

### Enterprise nursing-learning platform

Likely center of gravity: dean/executive academic leadership + central university procurement/IT + many program stakeholders.

The buying system becomes more complex but the strategic value can increase because the platform can standardize across programs/campuses.

## Institutional technology gate

Public university procurement policies make a structural point that matters for Elsevier: **academic willingness is necessary but not sufficient.**

Software used for teaching can trigger reviews for:

- cybersecurity and vendor risk;
- data privacy/classification;
- accessibility;
- LMS/SSO/integration architecture;
- duplicate institutional capability;
- legal terms;
- competitive procurement thresholds;
- payment/student-fee structure.

KU, for example, requires Procurement and IT review of software purchases regardless of cost or funding source; the University at Buffalo describes a process involving finance, accessibility, CIO/security/integration and purchasing functions beginning as early as faculty identification of a need.

For AI simulation products, these institutional gates are likely to become more important, not less.

## A better account map for Shadow Health

For each institution, capture these fields rather than only 'decision maker':

### Program topology

- institution type;
- degree programs using/relevant to Shadow Health;
- campuses/locations;
- whether curriculum is central or local;
- states/countries/regulators involved;
- cohort size.

### Clinical/simulation topology

- clinical placement model;
- major health-system partners;
- placement scarcity/constraint;
- simulation-center structure;
- simulation director/leader;
- virtual simulation maturity.

### Decision topology

- executive sponsor;
- economic buyer;
- program/curriculum owner;
- course-level champions;
- simulation owner;
- clinical placement owner;
- assessment/accreditation leader;
- IT/security/accessibility gatekeepers;
- procurement/legal path;
- implementation owner;
- renewal owner.

### Economic topology

- institutional budget versus student-paid access;
- contract level: course/program/school/system;
- funding source;
- renewal cadence;
- incumbent/adjacent platforms.

## Strategic product implication

Shadow Health's growth opportunity is not simply to convince more deans that simulation matters. It is to make the product legible and valuable to **each decision right in the buying system** while reducing the coordination cost among them.

That points toward product capabilities such as:

- curricular mapping for faculty;
- competency/outcomes evidence for program leaders;
- implementation and debrief support for simulation educators;
- cross-course/campus analytics for deans;
- accessibility/security/data documentation for institutional reviewers;
- clear student-cost and licensing models for finance/procurement.

A product that solves the learner experience but increases institutional coordination burden can still lose.
