# Nursing learning journey and simulation map — 2026

## Why this map exists

To understand where a simulation product belongs, start with the full learning system a school is operating. A nursing program does not primarily buy "simulations." It is trying to produce graduates who can demonstrate the competencies expected of their level of preparation while meeting accreditation, regulatory and institutional requirements.

Simulation is one learning environment inside that system. Its role changes as the learner develops.

## The end-to-end learner journey

### 0. Decide on nursing and choose a pathway

Before admission, the learner is choosing among paths such as associate-degree RN preparation, traditional/upper-division BSN, accelerated BSN, graduate-entry prelicensure nursing, or—if already an RN—advanced nursing education.

The program's early job is selection and expectation setting: prerequisites, prior degree requirements, healthcare experience where applicable, technical standards, costs, schedule intensity, clinical travel, and professional outcomes.

**Simulation fit:** mostly marketing/preview/orientation rather than core instruction. A realistic sample encounter can help the learner understand what nursing education and clinical reasoning feel like.

### 1. Complete prerequisites and enter the professional nursing sequence

Entry-level programs commonly require anatomy/physiology, microbiology, chemistry or other sciences, statistics, lifespan development and general education before or alongside the nursing core. Programs may also require prior healthcare exposure, background checks, health/immunization clearance, CPR and other clinical-compliance steps.

The key transition is from learning *about* biological and social systems into learning to think and act as a nurse.

**Simulation fit:** low-to-moderate initially; orientation and very early communication/observation cases can begin professional identity formation.

### 2. Foundations: assessment, safety and basic nursing practice

The learner starts combining professional nursing concepts with health assessment, foundational skills, pharmacology/pathophysiology and basic patient care.

The environments now split into distinct roles:

- classroom/content for knowledge;
- skills lab for discrete procedures and equipment handling;
- simulation for integrated assessment, communication, safety and judgment;
- early clinical experiences for real patients and workflow.

**Simulation fit: very high.** This is where standardized practice is safer and more controllable than immediately depending on patient availability. Strong use cases include interviewing, head-to-toe assessment, medication safety, recognition of abnormal findings, handoff, empathy and basic prioritization.

### 3. Adult illness and increasing complexity

The learner moves into medical-surgical/adult care and begins caring for patients with chronic and acute illness. They must connect pathophysiology, medications, assessment data, interventions and changing patient states.

Real clinical rotations become more central, but what any student sees is variable.

**Simulation fit: very high.** Standardized cases can ensure exposure to deterioration, competing cues, time pressure, escalation, documentation, delegation and conditions a learner may not encounter during a placement. This is also where repeat practice can expose the difference between knowing an answer and noticing/acting correctly in context.

### 4. Specialty and population contexts

Typical prelicensure sequences include mental/behavioral health, pediatrics, maternity/perinatal care, community/public health and sometimes higher-acuity experiences.

Clinical availability becomes particularly uneven: a learner may not see a birth, pediatric emergency, psychiatric crisis or other target event during the rotation.

**Simulation fit: very high for coverage gaps and high-risk/low-frequency cases.** Standardized patients are especially useful for communication-heavy experiences; manikin/VR/lab modalities can add procedural and physiologic realism.

### 5. Integration across multiple patients, teams and systems

As learners approach completion, the job changes from "can you perform this skill?" or "can you reason through this patient?" to "can you manage nursing work?"

That includes prioritization across patients, delegation, handoffs, interprofessional communication, documentation, safety, resource constraints and shifting clinical conditions.

**Simulation fit: high but should become more integrated and less tutorialized.** Multi-patient scenarios, unfolding cases, interprofessional simulation and less-prompted virtual patients are appropriate. The most valuable evidence is increasingly about judgment and prioritization, not completion of isolated steps.

### 6. Capstone / synthesis / transition-to-practice

Most entry-level programs culminate in a senior practicum, synthesis clinical, preceptorship or transition-to-practice experience plus licensure preparation. The learner is expected to operate with increasing independence while still supervised.

The final clinical/preceptor relationship exposes the learner to authentic workload and professional responsibility.

**Simulation fit: targeted.** It should fill exposure gaps, test readiness, support remediation and rehearse high-consequence scenarios—not crowd out the real-world transition experience. A standardized end-of-program assessment can be useful if it produces trustworthy, competency-linked evidence.

### 7. Graduation and licensure boundary

The school verifies degree completion and program outcomes; a prelicensure graduate then moves into the state licensure/NCLEX process and employment. Programs care about graduation, retention, NCLEX performance, employer confidence and practice readiness because those outcomes feed program evaluation and accreditation.

**Simulation fit:** primarily evidence and transition support rather than degree completion itself.

### 8. Advanced nursing education

For an RN entering an MSN, DNP or post-graduate advanced-practice pathway, the learner starts from an existing professional nursing base. APRN education typically adds advanced pathophysiology, pharmacology and health assessment, then specialty-specific diagnosis/management and supervised clinical practice.

**Simulation fit changes sharply:**

- comprehensive and focused advanced assessment;
- differential diagnosis and clinical reasoning;
- ordering/interpreting diagnostics;
- pharmacologic/non-pharmacologic management;
- procedures and task training;
- complex communication and shared decisions;
- telehealth;
- uncommon/high-risk specialty presentations;
- OSCE-style performance assessment;
- onsite immersion for otherwise-online programs.

The point is no longer "learn how to be a nurse" but "demonstrate advanced role-specific judgment and management."

## How major program types differ

| Program archetype | Typical learner on entry | Program shape | Simulation's most natural job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ADN / associate RN | College learner entering initial nursing preparation | General education/sciences plus sequenced nursing core and clinicals | Foundational practice, clinical judgment, specialty coverage, readiness for RN practice |
| Traditional / upper-division BSN | Pre-nursing learner after prerequisites | Broad generalist RN preparation, commonly two years of nursing sequence after prerequisites | Longitudinal integration from assessment/fundamentals through specialty and transition-to-practice |
| Accelerated BSN | Prior non-nursing bachelor's degree | Compressed continuous entry-level curriculum | Same competencies as BSN, but rapid feedback and reliable standardized practice become especially valuable |
| Graduate-entry prelicensure MSN | Prior non-nursing bachelor's degree | Intensive generalist RN preparation inside graduate degree | Similar to ABSN/BSN for entry-to-practice, often with explicit competency-based and leadership framing |
| RN-to-BSN | Already licensed RN | Completion curriculum emphasizing broader professional competencies | Lower direct-care need; role depends on population health, communication, systems, leadership and assessment goals |
| MSN/DNP APRN | Licensed RN | Advanced core + specialty didactic + staged supervised practica/preceptorship | Advanced assessment, diagnosis/management, OSCE, procedures, rare cases, telehealth, specialty readiness |
| Non-APRN graduate nursing | Licensed nurse entering leadership/informatics/education/etc. | Role-specific advanced competencies and practice experiences | Selective; useful when simulation directly represents role work rather than generic patient encounters |

## The staff journey behind the learner journey

### A. Program architecture and accreditation

**Primary actors:** dean/associate dean, program director, specialty directors, curriculum committee, assessment/accreditation leaders, faculty.

They define outcomes, map national competencies and specialty requirements into the curriculum, set progression standards, allocate resources and maintain evidence for accreditation/regulators.

**Simulation-product job:** make it easy to show exactly which competencies an activity develops or assesses and how performance evidence rolls up across courses and cohorts.

### B. Course and term planning

**Primary actors:** course coordinator, faculty, simulation educators, clinical-placement team, program administration.

They decide what content and practice belongs in a course, which experiences require real patients, which can or should be simulated, how students are grouped, and how the schedule works.

Physical simulation adds operational constraints: rooms, equipment, standardized patients, faculty/facilitators, AV systems and debrief spaces. Virtual simulation removes some constraints but creates others around licenses, rosters, LMS setup, assignment design and support.

**Simulation-product job:** lower the planning and configuration cost without forcing faculty to redesign the course around the product.

### C. Clinical-placement operations

**Primary actors:** clinical-placement specialists/coordinators, contracts/compliance staff, program/specialty faculty, students, preceptors and clinical sites.

Placement teams identify sites and preceptors, negotiate contracts, match course objectives and learner needs, manage onboarding/compliance, handle disruptions and ensure required experiences/hours are tracked.

This is a major hidden system. Johns Hopkins reports placing more than 1,200 students per semester, illustrating how operationally heavy placement can be.

**Simulation-product job:** do not assume the school wants to replace clinical simply because placement is difficult. The better question is which educational outcomes are unreliable or disproportionately expensive to obtain through placement and can be guaranteed through high-quality simulation.

### D. Simulation design and operations

**Primary actors:** simulation director/associate dean, simulation educators, course faculty, operations/technology specialists, standardized-patient staff, student workers/assistants.

The work includes aligning objectives, choosing modality, preparing learners, scheduling, configuring environments, running the event, facilitating, debriefing, maintaining equipment/software and preserving quality standards.

**Simulation-product job:** support the full event lifecycle—prebrief, scenario, facilitation, debrief, assessment and iteration—not only the learner-facing encounter.

### E. Teaching, supervision and feedback

**Primary actors:** didactic faculty, clinical faculty, course coordinator, preceptor, simulation educator.

Faculty retain responsibility for academic evaluation even when preceptors provide direct observation. They triangulate exams/assignments, simulation evidence and real-clinical performance to decide whether the learner is progressing.

**Simulation-product job:** produce evidence faculty can interpret, not opaque scores. The evidence should support coaching, remediation and progression decisions while preserving faculty judgment.

### F. Remediation and progression

When a learner struggles, the program has to identify whether the gap is knowledge, assessment, communication, reasoning, skill execution, prioritization, professionalism or something else; provide targeted support; then determine whether the learner can progress.

**Simulation-product job:** repeatable targeted practice is unusually valuable here. A strong system should connect observed gaps to specific remediation and then show whether performance changed.

### G. Program evaluation

Programs monitor graduation/retention, student outcomes, clinical competency, licensure performance, practice readiness, faculty/resource adequacy and stakeholder feedback.

**Simulation-product job:** longitudinal evidence should be legible at student, course, cohort and program levels. The highest-value analytics answer "where are learners systematically failing to become ready?" rather than simply "who completed the assignment?"

## What this implies for Shadow Health

Shadow Health is naturally strongest in the middle of the learning loop:

**prepare → encounter → reveal reasoning/behavior → feedback → repeat/remediate**

Its strategic opportunity is to connect that loop more explicitly to the larger program system.

### Strongest current fit

1. **Foundations and health assessment:** repeatable patient interviewing, objective/subjective data collection, communication and documentation.
2. **Adult and specialty courses:** guaranteed exposure to cases that may be variable or absent in clinical placement.
3. **Clinical judgment development:** unfolding information, cue recognition, prioritization and decisions.
4. **Advanced-practice education:** assessment, diagnostics, management and standardized patient/OSCE-like work.
5. **Remediation:** safe repetition of a specific competency gap.

### Product opportunities suggested by the program arc

- **Longitudinal learner model:** track demonstrated behaviors across courses rather than treating each DCE as an isolated assignment.
- **Competency map:** let faculty see how assignments contribute to AACN Level 1/Level 2 progression indicators and local program outcomes.
- **Placement-gap planning:** help programs identify where simulation should guarantee exposure without implying that all clinical time is substitutable.
- **Course-to-program analytics:** move from assignment completion toward evidence of cohort-level readiness and recurring curriculum gaps.
- **Faculty workflow:** make prebriefing, facilitation, debriefing, remediation and grading easier, not just the student encounter.
- **Cross-modality evidence:** if Shadow Health spans screen-based DCEs, lab and VR, normalize the evidence around competencies rather than around modality.
- **Transition-to-practice signal:** explore whether late-program simulations can add a trustworthy readiness signal when combined with real clinical evaluation.

## Important boundary

The most dangerous product bias is to treat more simulation as inherently better. Real clinical practice supplies things simulation cannot fully reproduce: actual patient responsibility, workflow friction, team dynamics, institutional systems, unpredictability and professional socialization.

The better strategy is **purposeful substitution and amplification**: use simulation where it creates more reliable learning evidence or exposure, then connect that learning back into real practice.

## Questions to carry into Shadow Health discovery

- At what point in each major customer curriculum is Shadow Health introduced, and why that point?
- Is it primarily replacing another learning activity, supplementing it, or being counted toward clinical experience?
- Which competencies do faculty believe Shadow Health teaches well enough to trust?
- Which competencies do they refuse to infer from virtual performance?
- How do course faculty currently combine Shadow Health results with exams, physical simulation and clinical/preceptor evaluations?
- When a learner performs poorly in Shadow Health, what remediation actually happens?
- Do program leaders ever inspect performance longitudinally across multiple courses, or is the data trapped at course/assignment level?
- Which clinical-placement gaps are programs most desperate to guarantee through simulation?
- In graduate/APRN programs, where does virtual simulation fit relative to standardized patients and OSCEs?
- What evidence would a dean or program director need to say that simulation improved practice readiness rather than simply engagement or confidence?
